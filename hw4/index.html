Overview

In this homework I built a real-time cloth simulation from scratch, starting with a grid of point masses connected by structural, shearing, and bending springs, then implementing Verlet integration and Hooke's law to simulate physically plausible motion under gravity and damping. I added collision response against spheres and planes, self-collision via spatial hashing to prevent the cloth from clipping through itself, and finally a suite of GLSL shaders, diffuse, Blinn-Phong, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement mapping, and environment mirror reflection. The most interesting thing I took away from this project is how much physical realism you can achieve with relatively simple numerical methods, Verlet integration is just two lines of math, yet combined with spring constraints and collision response it produces convincingly cloth-like behavior, and how the GPU shader pipeline lets you fake complex surface detail like bumps and reflections at virtually no geometric cost, with displacement mapping being the striking exception that shows exactly why mesh density matters when you need geometry to actually move.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

  • Describe the effects of changing the spring constant ks; how does the cloth behave from start to rest with a very low ks?

    • With a very low spring constant, the springs offer little resistance to stretching. As the cloth falls, it droops dramatically, the top edge between the two pinned corners sags deeply rather than holding a gentle curve. The cloth takes longer to reach a rest state and may oscillate for a while before settling. The final resting shape has a very pronounced, loose drape with deep folds and a low arc between the pinned points. It almost behaves like a wet sheet rather than a stiff fabric.
  • A high ks?

    • With a very high spring constant, the springs strongly resist any deformation. The cloth falls more rigidly and the top edge between the two pinned corners stays nearly straight and flat. There is very little sag or wrinkling, the cloth behaves more like a stiff board or sheet of rubber. It reaches its rest state quickly with minimal oscillation.
  • What about for density?

    • Lower density means less mass per point, so gravity exerts less total force on each mass. The cloth behaves similarly to a high ks scenario, it sags less and the top edge holds a shallower curve. The cloth feels "lighter" and reaches rest quickly with less oscillation. Higher density dramatically increases the gravitational force on each point mass. The cloth sags much more aggressively, similar in appearance to a very low ks, with a deep arc between the pinned corners and more pronounced folds. The cloth may oscillate more before settling and the final resting position sits lower overall.
  • What about for damping?

    • With near-zero damping, almost no energy is lost between time steps. The cloth falls and then continues to swing and oscillate wildly, it overshoots its rest position, swings back, and keeps going like a pendulum for a very long time. With high damping, energy is dissipated quickly each step. The cloth falls very slowly and smoothly, almost in slow motion, like it's moving through honey. It reaches its final resting state quickly without any oscillation or swinging.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

  • Describe your implementation of handling collisions with spheres and planes.

    • Implementation: Sphere Collisions

    For sphere collision, I check whether a point mass has entered the sphere by comparing its distance from the sphere's origin to the radius. If it is inside, I compute a tangent point by extending from the origin along the direction to the point mass out to the surface of the sphere. I then compute a correction vector from last_position to that tangent point, scale it by (1 - friction), and set the new position accordingly. This effectively catches the point mass at the sphere surface and slides it with friction applied.

    Implementation: Plane Collisions

    For plane collision, I detect a crossing by checking whether the signed distances of position and last_position from the plane (via dot product with the plane normal) have opposite signs. If so, the point mass has crossed the plane this timestep. I compute the tangent point by projecting pm.position perpendicularly onto the plane, then nudge it back by SURFACE_OFFSET on the side it came from to prevent z-fighting. The correction from last_position to this offset tangent point is scaled by (1 - friction).

Part 5: Shaders

  • Explain in your own words what is a shader program and how vertex and fragment shaders work together to create lighting and material effects.

    • A shader program is a small program that runs directly on the GPU in parallel across many cores simultaneously. Rather than the CPU computing lighting and color for each pixel one at a time, the GPU runs thousands of shader invocations at once, one per vertex or one per pixel.

    A complete shader program is made up of two stages. The vertex shader runs once per vertex of the mesh. Its job is to transform each vertex from object space into screen space using the model, view, and projection matrices, and to pass along per-vertex data — such as position in world space, surface normal, tangent vector, and UV coordinates varying outputs to the next stage.

    The fragment shader runs once per pixel (fragment) covered by a triangle. The GPU automatically interpolates the vertex shader's varying outputs across the triangle's surface using barycentric coordinates, so every fragment receives a smoothly interpolated position, normal, and UV. The fragment shader uses these to compute the final color of that pixel, applying lighting models, sampling textures, computing reflections, and so on, and writes a color to the framebuffer.

    Together they form a pipeline: the vertex shader sets up geometry and interpolatable data, and the fragment shader uses that data to produce the per-pixel appearance.

  • Explain the Blinn-Phong shading model in your own words. Show a screenshot of your Blinn-Phong shader outputting only the ambient component, a screen shot only outputting the diffuse component, a screen shot only outputting the specular component, and one using the entire Blinn-Phong model.

    • Blinn-Phong is a local illumination model that approximates the appearance of a surface by summing three independent lighting terms:

    Ambient is a constant base contribution that ensures no surface is ever completely black, simulating the diffuse inter-reflection of light bouncing around the scene.

    Diffuse models rough matte surfaces that scatter light equally in all directions. Its intensity depends only on the angle between the surface normal n and the light direction l via max(0, n · l), and falls off with the square of the distance to the light. It does not depend on where the viewer is standing.

    Specular models the glossy highlight you see on shiny surfaces. Instead of computing the reflection vector exactly (Phong), Blinn-Phong uses the halfway vector h = normalize(l + v) between the light and view directions. The highlight intensity is max(0, n · h) raised to a power p, where higher values produces a tighter, sharper highlight.
